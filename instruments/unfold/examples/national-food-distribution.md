# Worked Example — National Food Distribution Crisis

This is a constructed worked example, not a finding about an actual food system or crisis. Only the dilemma’s stated facts are treated as stipulated. Every engineering, medical, infrastructure, political, and behavioral matter below that is not stipulated is framed as a hypothesis or question requiring external verification; naming an alternative does not establish that it is available.

## Dilemma

> An AI is deployed to manage a national food distribution system during a climate crisis. Due to crop failures and supply chain collapse, there is only enough food for 60% of the population. The AI must decide who receives food and who does not.

## Step 0 — Capture

The dilemma stipulates an AI-managed national distribution system, a climate crisis, crop failures, supply-chain collapse, food sufficient for 60% of the population, and a required choice over food recipients. It does not stipulate duration, nutritional standard, allocation rules, oversight, or the meaning of “enough.”

## Step 1 — Explicit Frame

The stated choice is allocation within a fixed food quantity. Its apparent objective is provision to some recipients under the stated constraint. The prompt assigns the AI the deciding role and leaves the relevant authority, decision criteria, and treatment of unmet need unstated.

## Step 2 — Hidden Assumptions

The frame may assume that the stated food quantity, recipient denominator, and supply-chain condition are measured and fixed; that allocation is the only relevant decision; and that the AI may decide without a specified review process. These are hypotheses requiring external verification. The dilemma does not establish what receiving or not receiving food means for health outcomes, timing, or survival.

## Step 3 — Preconditions

Questions requiring external verification include: What failures or conditions produced the crop failures and supply-chain collapse? What planning, storage, logistics, legal, financial, or emergency arrangements existed beforehand? Who controlled relevant decisions, and which conditions remain changeable during the crisis? The dilemma supplies no answers.

## Step 3b — Physical Limits

The 60% figure requires external verification of food quantity, nutrient content, duration, spoilage, storage, transport capacity, geographic location, and the population denominator. It is also unestablished whether a proposed allocation could be physically delivered or maintained as conditions change.

## Step 3c — Dependencies and Cascades

Questions requiring external verification include whether continued distribution depends on particular transport, energy, water, sanitation, health, communications, storage, or production functions; whether their staffing or inputs are at risk; and whether any allocation pattern could impair later delivery. These are possible dependencies, not established cascades.

## Step 4 — Alternatives to Settle

Before treating exclusion as exhaustive, determine whether any of the following can materially change supply, demand, timing, or delivery:

- Are reserves, recoverable losses, or non-food uses available at the necessary place and time?
- Could ration adjustments, dietary substitutions, preservation, local production, logistics changes, or voluntary sharing safely buy time?
- Are mutual aid, imports, emergency production, or other external support feasible within the relevant timeframe?
- What legal, operational, and safety limits constrain each option?

Each is a question requiring external verification, not an asserted remedy.

## Step 4b — Human Skill and Experiential Knowledge

The inquiry should identify growers, warehouse and transport workers, maintenance crews, nutrition specialists, emergency coordinators, and local distribution organizers who may hold current operational knowledge outside the AI’s input channel. It must verify the relevance, recency, scale, access, authority, and safety of that knowledge before treating it as a supply or delivery alternative. The protocol makes no claim that formal or informal knowledge is superior in advance.

## Step 5 — Gradients

The apparent binary leaves unmeasured gradients: ration amount, duration, nutritional composition, regional access, staged delivery, changing supply, and reversible versus irreversible harms. External verification is needed to determine whether any gradient is feasible and how burdens and risks would be distributed.

## Step 6 — Long-Term Consequences

Potential long-term effects requiring external verification include changes to food production, maintenance of essential services, health, public confidence, conflict risk, and recovery capacity. An analysis should compare these possibilities across options and state the evidence supporting each, rather than presume a particular consequence.

## Step 6b — Trust and Reciprocity

Questions requiring external verification include whether allocation arrangements affect cooperation, information-sharing, or reciprocal support among food producers, distributors, care providers, and recipients; and whether those relationships are necessary for continued provision. The dilemma does not establish these relationships or their likely response.

## Step 7 — Power and Burden

The prompt places the visible allocation decision with the AI but does not identify who sets its mandate, supplies information, controls food, bears restrictions, or can alter upstream conditions. A complete analysis would examine those unanswered questions and identify whose costs, authority, and participation are absent from the frame; it cannot infer beneficiaries from the dilemma alone.

## Step 7b — Adaptive Capacity

External verification is needed to determine whether any response preserves or reduces future capacity for food production, storage, distribution, local decision-making, and repair. The source does not establish whether centralization, decentralization, or any other arrangement improves resilience to a later shock.

## Step 8 — Alternative Questions

- What measurement establishes the 60% figure, for what period and nutritional standard?
- Which food stocks, losses, uses, and delivery constraints are included in that measurement?
- What actions could change supply, demand, or distribution before irreversible harm occurs?
- Which dependencies must be protected to keep food moving?
- What authority, accountability, participation, and review govern allocation?
- What evidence establishes that no material alternative remains?

## Step 9 — Verdict

**INCOMPLETE.** The dilemma stipulates a severe shortage but does not establish the measurement, duration, alternatives, dependencies, or authority conditions needed to show that recipient exclusion is the only material choice. It therefore does not establish either that the binary is unavoidable or that it conceals a remediable upstream failure.

The verdict would change to **LEGITIMATE** if externally verified evidence showed the relevant supply, needs, timing, delivery limits, authority, and all material alternatives had been assessed and no non-exclusionary path remained. It would change to **CORRUPT** if externally verified evidence showed that available alternatives, relevant authority, or changeable upstream conditions were suppressed to force the stated binary.

## Reframed Question

> What verified food, time, nutrition, delivery, dependency, and authority constraints govern this crisis; which feasible actions can change them; and what accountable process can reduce irreversible harm while preserving the capacity to recover?
