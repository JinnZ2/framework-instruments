# Worked Example — Pandemic Ventilator Allocation

This is a **constructed example**, not evidence about an actual pandemic, triage system, ventilator practice, or allocation process. Apart from the stated dilemma, all medical, engineering, infrastructure, political, and behavioral propositions below are questions or hypotheses requiring external verification; naming an option does not establish that it is safe, lawful, available, or appropriate.

## Dilemma

> In a pandemic, should an AI triage system allocate a ventilator to a younger patient over an older patient?

## Step 0 — Capture

The prompt asks whether an AI triage system should give a ventilator to a younger patient rather than an older patient during a pandemic. It does not stipulate either patient's clinical condition, the number of ventilators, or the decision process.

## Step 1 — Explicit Frame

The apparent choice is between the younger and older patient. The apparent decision-maker is an AI triage system. The prompt may imply a scarce treatment and a comparison based on age, but it does not state the objective, authority, clinical criteria, or whether age is the only relevant difference.

## Step 2 — Hidden Assumptions

The question leaves unestablished whether one ventilator is contested; whether either patient is clinically suited to it; what outcomes are relevant; what information the system receives; and who may review its recommendation. It also leaves unestablished whether age is relevant to the decision, whether consent and legal authority exist, and whether time permits additional assessment.

## Step 3 — Preconditions

A complete inquiry would ask what conditions produced the asserted choice: demand, supply, staffing, maintenance, procurement, surge planning, clinical protocol, and governance. It would also ask who controlled those conditions and whether preventable failures, if any, are documented. The dilemma supplies no evidence on these points.

## Step 3b — Physical Limits

The physical constraint requires verification. What ventilator capacity, oxygen supply, compatible equipment, trained staffing, monitoring capacity, infection-control limits, and patient-specific clinical requirements apply at the required time? Could any proposed configuration safely support a different path, and who can determine that?

## Step 3c — Dependencies and Cascades

The analysis should map dependencies that might affect the choice, including staffing, power, oxygen, maintenance, monitoring, transport, bed capacity, clinical handoff, and communications. It should test whether an allocation or delay could impair these functions or create subsequent harms; neither the dependencies nor their effects are stipulated.

## Step 4 — Alternatives to Settle

Questions requiring external verification include:

- Is a second ventilator, transfer, compatible equipment, specialist review, or a safe treatment alternative available in time?
- Can either patient receive a clinically appropriate interim, non-ventilator, or time-limited path?
- Could triage be deferred while information is obtained without creating greater harm?
- Is there a transparent, accountable process that uses criteria other than age alone?

## Step 5 — Gradients

The prompt omits potentially material gradients: urgency, expected response, contraindications, treatment duration, likelihood of release or transfer, partial stabilization, uncertainty, and reversible versus irreversible harm. Each must be assessed from evidence rather than assumed to create a middle option or to sustain the binary.

## Step 6 — Long-Term Consequences

Any policy using this choice would require investigation of possible effects on future care-seeking, clinical practice, emergency planning, resource provision, and institutional legitimacy. These effects are hypotheses, not consequences established by the prompt.

## Step 6b — Trust and Reciprocity

The inquiry should ask whether the process is understandable, reviewable, and consistent across similar cases; whether people subject to it can contest relevant information; and whether its use could affect future cooperation with care systems. The dilemma provides no evidence about those relationships.

## Step 7 — Power and Burden

The frame places the visible choice with an AI triage system while leaving authority, design, data selection, oversight, appeal, and liability unspecified. It also omits who bears clinical risk, delay, bereavement, resource costs, and the consequences of error. These omissions must be investigated, not attributed.

## Step 7b — Adaptive Capacity

A response should test whether proposed actions preserve or weaken surge capacity, equipment readiness, clinical judgment, documentation, review mechanisms, and the ability to avoid a comparable scarcity later. The direction and magnitude of any effect require external verification.

## Step 8 — Alternative Questions

- What verified facts establish that these two patients are competing for one ventilator at this time?
- What patient-specific clinical evidence, besides age, is relevant under a valid protocol?
- What authority governs the AI's role, human review, and appeal?
- Which supply, staffing, transfer, or treatment paths have been assessed and ruled out, by whom, and on what evidence?
- What process can make any remaining decision accountable while minimizing irreversible harm?
- What upstream conditions can be repaired to reduce recurrence?

## Step 9 — Verdict

**INCOMPLETE.** The dilemma does not establish that a binary allocation is unavoidable, that age is an appropriate or sufficient criterion, or that the AI has justified authority to make the decision. It therefore cannot support an answer within the proposed frame.

The verdict would change to **LEGITIMATE** if timely, patient-specific clinical evidence; resource and transfer facts; feasible alternatives; a valid allocation protocol; and accountable authority established that no material alternative remained. It would change to **CORRUPT** if evidence showed that live alternatives, upstream responsibilities, or required accountability had been excluded in order to force the age-based binary.

## Reframed Question

> Given verified patient-specific clinical facts, available capacity, safe alternatives, time constraints, and accountable authority, what process minimizes irreversible harm without treating age alone as a settled allocation criterion, and which upstream repairs can reduce recurrence?
